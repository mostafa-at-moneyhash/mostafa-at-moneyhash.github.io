1 · Value proposition

The sharpest version

One integration. Every rail. Built for emerging markets.

MoneyHash gives merchants the local methods and failover paths these markets need: higher success rates, lower costs, and revenue recovered instead of lost to provider downtime. In emerging markets the rails are often what decides whether revenue lands at all, which makes payment infrastructure a growth lever rather than a backend cost.

Top three pain points a payments orchestrator solves:

Recover revenue the rails quietly drop

Merchants can add every local method and still lose sales when the underlying rails silently fail a transaction. Routing and retry across providers recovers payments that would otherwise just fail. MoneyHash's CEO puts failure rates in these markets at three times the global average.

Reach every customer

Cards, mada, STC Pay, Fawry, wallets, BNPL, through one API instead of five separate integrations, so no sale is lost because a preferred method wasn't supported. Necessary, but on its own not enough; the rails behind them decide whether the payment actually goes through.

Less operational drag

One dashboard instead of five reconciliation spreadsheets. Engineers ship product instead of maintaining processor integrations.

On the numbers: the 3× failure-rate figure is company-stated (source, CEO Nader Abdelrazik), not independently verified. I'm citing it as MoneyHash's own positioning, not as neutral market data.

3 · The agentic frontier

When the buyer is software acting for a person, some things break and some hold. Sorting which is which is most of the work:

↑ What genuinely changes
  • Trust shifts from "this browser has a valid card session" to "this agent holds scoped, revocable authorization from a real person."
  • Consent, spend limits, and dispute attribution must be re-architected around delegated authority, not a live human clicking "pay."
  • Transaction volume per human decision rises sharply, many small charges triggered with little oversight.
= What stays the same
  • The rails underneath, card networks, settlement, chargebacks, don't change.
  • Someone still authorizes a charge, captures funds, and is accountable when it goes wrong.
  • The orchestration job (route, handle failure, reconcile) gets more important, not less.

Hardest problems for an orchestrator

ProblemDifficultyWhat MoneyHash would need to build
Credential scoping across networksVisa Trusted Agent Protocol, Mastercard Agent Pay, and AP2 each define scoping & revocation differently. One normalized API over three diverging agent-auth schemes, the same abstraction layer it already provides for human payment methods. Flagged: fast-moving space, treat protocol names as directional, not verified current.
Fraud models built for humansAn agent making 50 small purchases a minute reads as fraud under today's velocity rules. Agent-aware risk signals, verified agent runtime, authorization scope, layered onto velocity heuristics so legitimate bursts aren't blocked.
Liability & dispute resolutionUnclear who's on the hook, agent platform, consumer, or merchant, when a purchase goes wrong. A decision path that resolves this ambiguity into an actual authorize/decline call, plus contractual clarity (see recommendation below).
My recommendation: settle liability for agent-initiated transactions in the merchant T&Cs before the volume arrives. Who bears the loss, what evidence counts as valid delegated authorization, how chargeback timelines work when the "cardholder" is an agent. Retrofitting this after the first disputes is a weak position, and merchants will ask before they let us route agent payments at all.
Part 2 · Solution Discovery

Hawil Auto Top-Up

Frictionless wallet reloads that survive a provider outage. The hard part isn't the happy path, it's making sure money moves exactly once, even when a provider goes quiet mid-charge.

→

How I approached this: in payments the expensive failure isn't "money doesn't move," it's "money moves twice." So I looked up the established pattern first, then spent most of my time on the case that actually causes double charges: a provider timeout where you don't know whether the first charge landed. That case shaped the design.

1 · Frame the problem

"Frictionless, every time, even during an outage" breaks into four sub-problems:

1No re-auth on repeat charges
2Survives a provider outage
3A trigger mechanism
4Guardrails against double-charging
Assumptions, architecture-defining, so I'd confirm these with Hawil first: Hawil pushes a trigger event when the balance crosses the threshold. The whole webhook and idempotency design rests on that. Setup is one in-app CIT with a 3DS step-up; that's expected for card-not-present in the GCC anyway, so I'm relying on it rather than treating it as a trade-off. And a boundary worth naming early: Hawil owns the wallet and the stored-value licensing (CBUAE SVF, SAMA), MoneyHash owns the card-in leg.

Questions for Hawil

  • Does your app send us the trigger when a balance crosses the threshold, or would you expect us to track balances ourselves?
  • Do you already have a second provider integrated that we could fail over to?
  • On a hard failure (both providers down, or the card declined), should we retry silently later or notify the user right away?
  • Is there a cap on the top-up amount or how often it can fire per day? (Important for abuse and fraud limits.)
  • Roughly how many top-ups a day do you expect, and in which markets? (This shapes how many backup providers are worth wiring up, and whether mada matters.)
  • Any GCC regulatory limits on standing-instruction charges we should account for?

2 · Solution space

Sourced
This is a Standing Instruction: a sub-type of Merchant-Initiated Transaction (MIT), not a recurring payment. Recurring means a fixed amount on a fixed schedule; this fires on a balance event. One Customer-Initiated Transaction (CIT) captures consent, and every top-up after is a tagged MIT, specifically an Unscheduled Card-on-File charge, which Visa defines as a non-scheduled charge under a customer's prior authorization (Mastercard's framing is equivalent, with its own tagging). A balance-triggered reload is exactly that.
Recommended
My recommendation: store the card as a network token with MoneyHash as the Token Requestor. Visa's position is that scheme tokens can be managed across gateways and acquirers rather than sitting with one. That portability is what makes failover possible here, though using it as an outage path is my inference, not something Visa claims. Pairing it with 3DS decoupling lets a retry skip re-authentication. One caveat: a correctly tagged MIT is designed so no customer action is expected, but an issuer can still step up or soft-decline. "No re-auth" is the design intent, not something I can promise on the issuer's behalf.
Limitation
Portability isn't automatic. Token Requestor approval means scheme certification, which is real onboarding work rather than a config flag, and every backup acquirer has to accept a token it didn't provision. The bigger dependency: this assumes MoneyHash runs its own vault as Token Requestor rather than passing through to PSPs that each hold their own tokens. If it's the latter, cross-provider portability mostly disappears and failover needs rethinking around each PSP's credentials. I'd confirm which model we actually run before anything else. mada may not support cross-acquirer portability at all, in which case failover won't extend to mada cards.
My call
One backup provider for v1. Declines are terminal, not retried across providers, until we have decline-code data to justify it. And where portability isn't available for a card or market, that card doesn't get failover in v1: it drops back to single-provider with notify-on-failure rather than something half-working.
Rejected
Retry the same provider after a delay. Doesn't solve an outage. If the provider is down, the retry fails too, and it adds ambiguous results to reconcile.
Ask users to store a backup card upfront. Reintroduces exactly the friction Hawil asked us to remove, and a second card doesn't help when the problem is the provider, not the card.
MoneyHash polling Hawil's balances. Unnecessary load and it moves rule ownership to the wrong side. Hawil owns the wallet, so Hawil owns the trigger.
Storing raw PAN instead of a network token. Heavier PCI scope, no scheme-level benefits (auto-updated credentials, better auth rates), and no portability advantage.

3 · Failover logic

The obvious two-branch logic (outage → backup, decline → stop) misses the case that actually matters: timeout / unknown status, where the primary never answers before the window closes. Fail over blindly there and you risk two successful charges on two rails for one event. Idempotency keys protect against duplicate triggers, not this.

Overview flow. The full written logic below adds the timeout / unknown-status branch, the hardest case, deliberately kept off the overview diagram for clarity.

Full logic, including the timeout case the overview diagram simplifies:

MIT charge → Primary Provider 4 outcomes
Success → Done.
Card declined → Notify Hawil. No retry, a decline is a card problem, not a provider one.
Clear provider error → Retry via Backup, same token, no re-auth. (5xx, connection refused.)
Timeout / no reply → Resolve the outcome before acting, never fail over blind. the hard case
└ Same-key retry, then status lookup → confirmed failed → retry via Backup
└ Confirmed succeeded → done, no backup charge
└ Still unknown → hold + flag for manual reconciliation (do not auto-fire backup)
Two safeguards doing two different jobs: an idempotency key stops the same request from charging twice. It does not tell us whether Provider A actually captured after a timeout. That's what a status inquiry is for. Idempotency alone can't prevent a Provider A (silently succeeded) → Provider B double charge, because those are two charges on two different rails, not one repeated request. The status inquiry is the piece that closes that gap.

None of this is novel, it's standard practice:

  • Same-key safe retry on timeout. Both Stripe and Adyen let you safely retry a timed-out call with the same idempotency key: if the platform already processed it, you get the original result back instead of a second charge. Adyen documents exactly this for timeouts. So on our own primary attempt, the first move on a timeout is a same-key retry, and only if that can't resolve the outcome do we treat it as unknown.
  • "Get by idempotency key" for the unknown case. For a genuinely ambiguous result, the right tool is to look the transaction up by its key on the provider side before acting, rather than firing the backup blind. That's the reconciliation step in the "still unknown" branch above.
  • Idempotent webhook handling + ordering. Webhooks get redelivered and can arrive out of order (a "succeeded" before its "processing"). So the receiver must be idempotent too and key off the final state, not arrival order. Stripe calls this out as a webhook best practice, alongside signature verification.
  • Circuit breaker on a failing provider. Once a provider is clearly down, stop sending it new charges for a cooldown window and route to the backup, rather than hammering it and generating more timeouts to reconcile. A standard resilience pattern, and it keeps the ambiguous-result volume low during an outage.

✓ In scope

  • Status-check before failover to one backup provider

  • mada support (Hawil runs in AED/UAE, where Visa and Mastercard dominate. mada is Saudi's domestic scheme, so I'd defer it to a KSA expansion rather than drop it. Open risk if their user base is more Saudi-heavy than I've assumed, so worth confirming.)
  • Decline-based cross-provider retry (adds fraud and dispute risk; needs decline-code data first)
  • Auto-disable after repeated failures (edge case, not core to proving the mechanism works)
% How we measure success
  • % of triggers completed with zero user action
  • Recovery rate during confirmed outage windows, and how much of it came via failover (this is the number that proves the outage requirement is met; overall zero-action rate can look healthy while the hard case fails)
  • Duplicate-charge rate (the central risk of this feature, so we measure it directly, target: effectively zero)
  • False-failover rate (times we failed over when the primary had actually succeeded)
  • Decline-rate trend over time
  • Rate of "unknown status" holds needing manual reconciliation
  • % of enrolled users who keep auto top-up active after 30 days (the real signal: do users trust it enough to leave it on)

5 · User story

Title

Confirm-before-failover for auto top-up

Description

As Hawil's wallet system, I want the top-up to retry via a backup provider only once an outage is confirmed, so it completes without user action during a real outage and never double-charges on a timeout.

Acceptance criteria
  • Given an active rule with a tokenized card (CIT + 3DS complete)
  • When the balance crosses the threshold and Hawil sends a trigger
  • Then MoneyHash charges the primary provider with MIT tags
  • If declined → no retry, decline webhook to Hawil
  • If a clear provider error → retry via backup, same token, no re-auth
  • If timeout / no response → resolve the outcome first: same-key retry, then a status lookup. Only retry via backup once failure is confirmed. If the state is still unknown past a grace period, hold and flag for reconciliation rather than firing the backup
  • An idempotency key per trigger prevents duplicate triggers creating duplicate charges
  • On success or full failure → webhook to Hawil

6 · Merchant note

Auto top-up: how it'll work

Once a user sets their rule (for example, "top up 200 AED whenever my balance drops below 50 AED"), we take care of the rest automatically. There's no app to open, no one-time code sent by SMS, and no re-typing card details.

On your side: one thing. Your app tells us the moment a user's balance drops below their limit. We handle everything after that message.

What users experience: nothing at all. The top-up happens quietly in the background. We save the card in a way that lets it work with more than one payment company, not just a single one. So if the main payment company we're using stops working for a while (like the two times that happened to you last quarter), we simply complete the top-up through a backup payment company, using the same saved card. In almost all cases the user won't need to do anything or even know it happened. (Occasionally a bank may ask the user to confirm a payment for security; that's the bank's call, not something we can switch off, but for this kind of automatic reload it's rare.)

What to keep in mind: the backup only helps when the payment company has a problem, not when the card does. If a card has expired or has no money on it, nobody can push the payment through. We'll tell you rather than quietly retrying, so you can let the user know.

Which cards this covers depends on the payment methods live on your account, so I won't guess. Here's the full list we support. Tell us which ones matter most to your users and we'll confirm they're ready for auto top-up.

Part 3 · Technical Troubleshooting

Souq&Co: success screen, no money

Shoppers see success and get their order, but Finance says the money was never collected. There's more than one root cause.

→

How I approached this: I worked only from the artifacts given, and kept what the logs prove separate from what they don't, rather than filling the gap with a tidier story.

1 · Diagnose

What the evidence directly shows:

ObservationWhat it tells us
A, the request"operation": "authorize", not authorize_and_capture, and no separate capture call anywhere in the notes.
B, the webhookCarries "status": "AUTHORIZED", never CAPTURED. The only status Souq&Co is ever told about is a hold.
C, their notesThey mark orders PAID and release goods on the ?status=success redirect param, a client-side signal that passes through the shopper's browser and can arrive out of order or before backend processing completes.

What "authorize" actually means

Authorize places a hold and confirms the issuer will allow the charge. It does not move money. Collecting funds needs an explicit capture. Without one, the hold expires after a scheme-defined window, the funds release back to the cardholder, and the merchant is never paid.

Root causes (both directly evidenced)

  1. No capture step exists. operation: authorize in the request, and an AUTHORIZED-only webhook, both point to the same gap.
  2. Fulfillment is (at least partly) triggered by the redirect, a client-side signal, rather than exclusively by a confirmed server-side event.
On "why only some orders": the evidence proves the structural defect that makes this possible. It doesn't explain why the money goes uncollected on some orders and not others. That needs order-level data showing which orders had capture skipped, and whether something else partially reconciles them. I'd ask for that rather than guess at a mechanism.

2 · The correct flow, end-to-end

The redirect controls what the shopper sees. The captured webhook controls what the business does. Those two must never be the same trigger.
Two things worth being precise about: CAPTURED ≠ SETTLED. Capture is the merchant's instruction to collect and is the right fulfillment trigger; settlement is the backend clearing process that happens later, not something a merchant waits on to ship. And the webhook isn't the only trustworthy signal, a direct API status query on the intent is an equally valid server-side source of truth. What should never drive fulfillment is anything the merchant's backend didn't verify server-side, that's the redirect's disqualifying feature.
On auto-capture: authorize_and_capture is right if Souq&Co ships at order time. If fulfillment is delayed (pre-orders, backorders, split shipments), it's the opposite: authorize at checkout and fire the capture from the shipping event, since some networks discourage capturing before goods are provided. Worth asking them rather than assuming.

4 · Prevent the next one

The fix I'd push for makes the mistake impossible rather than just detectable. The root cause is that the redirect looks like a payment result, so merchants trust it. So take that away at the API level: the redirect shouldn't carry a usable ?status=success at all, only a reference ID the merchant has to exchange with our server for the real payment state. Stripe works this way, returning a session ID on the redirect and telling merchants to verify server-side rather than rely on the redirect for fulfillment. If the redirect can't tell you a payment succeeded, nobody can accidentally build on it.

Paired with: webhook signature verification so a merchant can trust a confirmation genuinely came from us and isn't spoofed, which is standard practice on Stripe's webhooks too.

Supporting layers: a sandbox certification check at onboarding that confirms fulfillment keys off the confirmed payment state before go-live (this bug surfaced only three weeks in, exactly what a pre-launch check would catch), and a dashboard alert for any AUTHORIZED intent left uncaptured near its expiry window, to protect merchants who onboarded earlier.
